1. A byte wraps around

A register has a fixed number of bits, so it can hold only so many patterns: 16 at 4 bits, 256 at 8. Count up past the last one and you are back at zero. Count down past zero and you are back at the last one. The patterns sit on a wheel, not a line.

Subtracting B means stepping back B places around that wheel. If the steps never pass zero, the answer is the ordinary one. If they do, the register lands on a pattern near the top of the range, and that pattern is the result. Nothing has gone wrong: the hardware did the only thing it can do.

Outermost: the four bits of each pattern. Then its unsigned reading, then its signed reading. Amber dot: A. Red dot: A − B.

The wheel also explains why the same pattern has two readings. Going round the outside, the numbers run 0 up to 15. Going round the inside, the top half has been relabelled as negatives, so 15 is −1, 14 is −2, and so on. Stepping back from 3 by 5 lands on the pattern that is 14 to one reading and −2 to the other. The circuit does not know which label you are using, and does not need to.

2. Negating: NOT, then add 1

Stepping back B places is the same as stepping forward by the wheel's size minus B. At 4 bits, subtracting 5 is adding 11. At 8 bits, subtracting 5 is adding 251. So if the circuit can make 16 − B, or 256 − B, from B, it can subtract with nothing but an adder.

Inverting every bit of B gives 15 − B at 4 bits (255 − B at 8), because each column that held a 1 now holds a 0 and vice versa, and all-ones is 15. Adding 1 more makes it 16 − B, exactly the number to add. That is the whole trick: −B = NOT B + 1.

3. The adder does it

An adder already has a carry-in on bit 0. Feed NOT B into its B inputs and set that carry-in to 1, and it computes A + NOT B + 1, which is A − B. In hardware the inversion is one XOR gate per bit with a SUB wire on its other input: SUB low passes B through, SUB high inverts it and also drives the carry-in. The same adder adds and subtracts, which is why no CPU has a separate subtractor.

Tap bits in A or B to change the sum. The carries row shows the 1 entering bit 0 and the carry out leaving the top bit.

That last point is where the carry flag comes from. If a carry comes out of the top bit, the adder reached 16 (or 256) and dropped it, which cancels the 16 we added with NOT B + 1, so the result is the plain difference and no borrow happened. If no carry comes out, the 16 is still in there: the result wrapped, because B was bigger than A. ARM and most other designs store this carry out as C, so after a subtraction C = 1 means no borrow, C = 0 means a borrow. x86 stores the opposite and calls it the borrow flag, but the circuit is the same.

4. The same subtraction on paper

You do not have to think in bits to get the right pattern. Work in decimal, then fit the answer into the width, then convert with the top bit worth minus its place value.
