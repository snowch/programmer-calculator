Prerequisites

You should already be comfortable with:

  • Reading a number in binary and hex, and converting between them (e.g. 0b1010 = 0xA = 10).
  • The truth tables of NOT, AND, OR and XOR.
  • The idea that a CPU register holds a fixed number of bits (here 4 or 8).

Notation. Gate equations on this page use the usual Boolean shorthand: A ⊕ B is XOR (1 exactly when the inputs differ), A·B or AB is AND, A + B is OR when it appears inside a gate equation, and ¬A is NOT. So the full adder's carry-out, A·B + cin·(A ⊕ B), reads "A and B, or cin and (A xor B)". Everywhere else on the page, + is ordinary addition.

Self-check — if you can answer these without the lab, you're ready:

  1. What is 0b0110 AND 0b1010?
  2. What is the largest unsigned value an 8-bit register can hold?
  3. What does 1 XOR 1 give?
Answers

1. 0b0010.  2. 255 (0xFF).  3. 0.

You do not need to know what an ALU is, what a flag is, or anything about two's complement. The background section introduces all three.

Learning goals

By the end of this lab you will be able to:

  1. Explain why bitwise logic ops need no communication between bit positions (columns), while addition does.
  2. Trace a carry rippling through a chain of full adders and predict the result.
  3. Show how subtraction and negation are built from addition using invert-and-add-one.
  4. State what each of the N, Z, C and V flags means and which gates produce it.
  5. Distinguish unsigned overflow (C) from signed overflow (V), and predict both for a given addition.
  6. Describe what the three kinds of shift do to the bits and to the carry flag.
  7. Explain how two cross-coupled gates store a bit, and why a register copies its input only on a clock edge.

Background

What an ALU is. Inside every CPU there is one circuit whose whole job is to take one or two numbers and produce a new number from them: add, subtract, AND, shift and so on. It's called the arithmetic logic unit, or ALU. It has no memory and no clock of its own — it is pure combinational logic, a web of gates, so its output changes the moment its inputs do. Everything else in a CPU is plumbing to get numbers into the ALU and results out of it.

A register is just a row of bits the CPU holds on to — in this lab, A and B are 4 or 8 bits wide. An instruction like add selects which operation the ALU performs, the ALU computes, and the result is written back to a register. Alongside the result the ALU also outputs a few single bits of information about the result: was it zero, was it negative, did it overflow. These are the flags. They are what makes if statements and loops possible: a conditional branch simply tests a flag left by the previous ALU operation.

Where the operation comes from. The instruction is itself a number, fetched from memory into the instruction register. A few of its bits, the opcode, go to the control unit, which decodes them into the wires that tell the ALU what to do and which registers to use. So "add" never reaches the ALU as a word: it arrives as a pattern on the operation lines, and the ALU is wired so that pattern selects the adder. Two readings of the same bits. An 8-bit pattern has no intrinsic sign. Read as unsigned, 0xFF is 255. Read as two's complement signed, the top bit counts as −128, so 0xFF is −1 and the range is −128…127. The hardware adds the same way regardless; the flags let software decide whether the answer is trustworthy under the reading it cares about.

Negation. To negate a two's complement number, invert every bit and add 1. This is why an ALU never needs a subtractor: A − B is just A + (NOT B) + 1.

From gates to an ALU. Bitwise ops use one gate per bit position. Addition uses one full adder per bit position, and a full adder is only five gates: sum = A ⊕ B ⊕ cin and carry-out = A·B + cin·(A ⊕ B). Tap the inputs to watch them work.

The highlighted row is the current input; ⊕ is XOR. The first XOR and AND form a half adder; the rest fold in the carry.

Four of them add 4-bit numbers. One full adder adds a single pair of bits. To add two 4-bit numbers you use four of them, one per bit, and they work the way you add on paper: write the two numbers one above the other, add each pair of digits starting from the right, and carry a 1 into the next place whenever a pair adds up to 2 or more. Each place, called a column, gets its own full adder. Its A and B inputs are that column's two bits, its carry-in is whatever the column on its right carried out, and its carry-out feeds the column on its left. That carry wire is the only connection between the adders. The carry-in of the rightmost column is tied to 0, and the carry-out of the leftmost column becomes the C flag. Below, the four adders are drawn as boxes marked FA (short for full adder), with the same sum written out as long addition beside them. Tap one of the FA boxes to open it and see its five gates working on that column; press ripple to watch the carries settle one column at a time, which is why the top bit is always the last to be ready.

From gates to a register. Every gate so far forgets its inputs the moment they change; the full adder has no memory at all. Memory comes from feedback. Wire two NOR gates so that each one's output is an input of the other, and the pair has two stable states that it holds for as long as the power is on. An inverter and two AND gates in front turn the pair into a D latch: while enable is 1, Q copies D; the moment enable drops to 0, the latch keeps whatever it last saw. S = D·enable sets the pair and R = ¬D·enable resets it. With enable at 0 neither can fire, and the two NOR gates simply keep holding each other's output in place.

A register is a row of these cells, one per bit, sharing one enable line. Real CPUs use a sharper version, the edge-triggered D flip-flop: two latches back to back, arranged so that Q changes only at the instant the clock rises and never "sees through" while the clock is high. Four flip-flops with a shared clock and a load line make a 4-bit register, and the 74HC173 in the build section is exactly that. Set the D bits, then press the clock:

Between clock edges the D inputs can change freely; nothing reaches Q until the next edge. With load off, the edge is ignored. That is how a CPU writes one register and leaves all the others alone: every register sees the same clock, but only the chosen one has its load line raised.

The flags. After an operation the ALU records four one-bit facts, which conditional branches then test:

FlagMeaningBuilt from
Nresult is negative when read as signedresult's top bit, straight through
Zresult is zeroNOR of every result bit
Cunsigned result didn't fit (or a bit was shifted out)carry out of the top adder
Vsigned result didn't fitcarry into the top bit XOR carry out of it

This lab follows the ARM / Harris & Harris convention: after subtraction, C = 1 means no borrow was needed. x86 stores the opposite sense for subtraction, so if you later read Intel manuals, keep that in mind.

Try these

Guided exercises

For each one, predict the result and flags before you look. Each "Load" button sets the lab up for you.

1. Columns that don't talk

Flip bit 7 of A. Which result bits change? Now flip bit 0 of B. Which change? Why do no other columns react?

Answer

Only the same column changes each time. Each AND gate sees just its own pair of inputs — there is no wire between columns.

2. Masking and setting

Read the result in hex. Then switch to OR with the same inputs. What does AND with 0x0F do to a byte, and what does OR with 0x0F do?

Answer

AND keeps only the low nibble (0x05). OR forces the low nibble to all ones (0xBF). This is how software clears and sets individual bits.

3. Watch the ripple

Trace it by hand first: bit 0 is 1 + 1, which gives sum 0 and carry 1. Continue for each column. How far does the carry travel? Now set A to 15 and repeat.

Answer

7 + 1: the carry travels through bits 0–2 and stops at bit 3, giving 8. 15 + 1: it ripples through every column and out the top, so R = 0, Z = 1, C = 1. The top adder can't produce its answer until every adder to its right has.

4. Same bits, two overflows

Note C and V. Then load 0xFF + 1 from "Try these" and compare. In each case, which reading (signed or unsigned) gave a wrong answer?

Answer

0x7F + 1: unsigned 127 + 1 = 128 fits, C = 0; signed 127 + 1 should be 128 but the result reads −128, V = 1. 0xFF + 1: unsigned 255 + 1 doesn't fit, C = 1; signed −1 + 1 = 0 is correct, V = 0. The flags report on the two readings independently.

5. The V rule

Look at the two red carries feeding the V gate: carry into bit 7 and carry out of bit 7. Find inputs where both are 1 (V = 0), where only the out is 1, and where only the in is 1. State the rule in words.

Answer

V = 1 exactly when the carry into the sign bit differs from the carry out of it. Adding two positives that produce a negative (carry in only), or two negatives that produce a positive (carry out only), is overflow. If both carries agree the sign bit is still telling the truth.

6. Subtraction is addition in disguise

Find the inverters on the B inputs and the 1 on the first carry-in. Now swap to 3 − 5. What are R, N and C, and what does C mean here?

Answer

5 − 3 = 2 with C = 1 (no borrow). 3 − 5 = 0xFE, which is −2 signed, so N = 1; C = 0 because a borrow was needed. In ARM style C = 1 means "A ≥ B as unsigned".

7. The number that can't be negated

Negate 0x80. What comes out, and which flag tells you something went wrong? Why can this happen?

Answer

The result is 0x80 again, with V = 1. −128 negated should be +128, but the signed range only reaches 127. Two's complement has one more negative value than positive.

8. Three shifts

Compare SHR and ASR on 0x92. Which one is "divide by 2" for a signed number, and why does the other give the wrong answer? Then try SHL on 0x81 and explain where the top bit went.

Answer

0x92 is −110 signed. ASR copies the sign bit down and gives 0xC9 = −55 (correct). SHR fills with 0 and gives 0x49 = 73, correct only for the unsigned reading (146 ÷ 2). SHL on 0x81 pushes the 1 at bit 7 out into C.

Check yourself

Answer without the lab, then verify.

  1. Which single gate type, if you had enough of them, could build everything else on this page?
    Answer

    NAND (or NOR). Both are functionally complete.

  2. In 8-bit, what are R, N, Z, C and V after 0x40 + 0x40?
    Answer

    R = 0x80, N = 1, Z = 0, C = 0, V = 1 (64 + 64 = 128 overflows the signed range).

  3. A branch instruction wants "jump if A < B as signed numbers" after computing A − B. Which flags does it test?
    Answer

    N ≠ V. If no overflow happened, N alone says the result was negative; if overflow did happen, the sign is inverted, so N must be flipped. XOR-ing them handles both.

  4. And "jump if A < B as unsigned numbers"?
    Answer

    C = 0 under the ARM convention (a borrow was needed). Under x86 it would be C = 1.

  5. Why is the Z flag computed with a NOR rather than an OR?
    Answer

    OR of all bits is 1 when the result is non-zero. We want 1 when the result is zero, so the OR is inverted.

  6. In an 8-bit ripple-carry adder, which result bit is ready last, and what does that imply about speed?
    Answer

    Bit 7 and the carry flag, because they depend on the carry from every column before them. Adder delay grows with width, which is why real CPUs use carry-lookahead or similar.

Build it for real

If "register" and "ALU" still feel abstract, here is the smallest build that makes them physical, in 4 bits, using the same chips as Ben Eater's breadboard computer. You do not have to build it; reading the five steps is enough to see what the words mean. A bit is one wire sitting at 5 V or 0 V, shown by an LED. A register is a chip that remembers a few such wires. An ALU is a chip with no memory whose output wires are a pure function of its input wires.

  1. One full adder from five gates. Wire the circuit from the Background section with three chips: a 74HC86 (four XOR gates), a 74HC08 (four AND gates) and a 74HC32 (four OR gates). Three switches give A, B and carry-in, each with a 10 kΩ resistor to ground so an open switch reads 0; two LEDs show sum and carry-out. Flick through all eight rows of the truth table. You have built the box the lab draws as FA.
  2. Four of them make the adder. Chain four full adders, each carry-out into the next carry-in and the first carry-in to ground, and you have the ripple-carry adder the lab draws: that is two 74HC86, two 74HC08 and one 74HC32. Or buy the same four adders in one chip, the 74HC283. Either way, two 4-way DIP switches feed A and B, and five LEDs show the sums and the carry-out. Set A to 0111 and B to 0001: the sum LEDs show 1000 the instant the switch closes. There is no clock and no "run"; the outputs just follow the inputs, which is all "ALU" means. Set A to 1111 and B to 0001 and the sums go dark while the carry-out LED lights. That LED is the C flag, and on the 74HC283 it is literally pin 9. The LED on S3 is N. The DIP switches are registers you load by hand.
  3. Subtraction and the other flags. Insert a 74HC86 between the B switches and the adder, with a SUB switch on the second input of every gate and on the adder's carry-in. SUB off passes B through; SUB on inverts B and the carry-in adds the 1, so the adder computes A + NOT B + 1, which is A − B. For Z, feed the four sum lines into one gate of a 74HC4002 (a 4-input NOR) and light an LED from its output. For V, overflow means the two numbers entering the adder had the same top bit and the sum's top bit differs: with spare XOR gates and one AND gate from a 74HC08, compute XNOR(A3, B′3) AND XOR(A3, S3), where B′3 is the bit-3 XOR output, the operand the adder actually sees.
  4. Registers: the numbers stay put. Put a 74HC173, a 4-bit D register, between each DIP switch and the adder: switches into its D inputs, its Q outputs into the adder and four LEDs, and one push button on the clock pin of both chips. Set the switches to 0111 and press the button: the register LEDs show 0111. Now flip the switches to anything else; the register LEDs do not move. The chip is remembering, and that is the whole of what a register is. Its load-enable pins decide whether a press changes it. To see the memory in pure gates first, wire the D latch from the Background section with a 74HC02 (four NOR gates), a 74HC08 and a 74HC04 inverter; the 74HC173 is four sharper versions of that cell. The adder now works on "registers A and B", the block diagram at the top of this page, and in this build you are the control unit: the SUB switch is an operation line, and each press of the clock executes one instruction.
  5. The payoff: an accumulator. Move four jumpers so the adder's sum outputs feed register A's D inputs instead of the switches. Load B with 0001. Each press of the button now performs A ← A + B and the A LEDs count 0000, 0001, 0010 and so on. Load B with 0011 and it counts in threes; switch SUB on and it counts down. That is one CPU instruction, ADD A, B, per button press, and "the result is written back to a register" stops being a metaphor. Debounce the button first, for instance with a 74HC14 Schmitt inverter and a 10 kΩ resistor with a 1 µF capacitor, or one press adds several times.

What bites beginners

  • CMOS inputs must never float: a pull-down resistor on every switch input, and unused gate inputs tied to ground.
  • A 0.1 µF capacitor across each chip's power pins, close to the chip.
  • The 74HC283 computes its carries with lookahead logic, so you will not see a ripple on it. To watch the ripple the lab draws, use the four gate-built adders from step 2.
